## What do you wish your supervisor understood?

The four situations that come up in every room where this gets discussed:

- **The feedback lottery.** Some meetings you get gold; some you get "looks fine" on a chapter you sweated over. The variance isn't random — it's steerable.
- **The vanishing supervisor.** Three weeks for an email reply while your milestone clock keeps ticking. Waiting silently is the worst available strategy.
- **The mind-reading trap.** You expect direction; they expect independence; nobody ever said either out loud. Most friction is mismatched, unspoken expectations.
- **The power asymmetry.** It's hard to push back on the person who examines your progress. But polite pushback is a skill they expect a PhD to develop — often without ever saying so.

## Make the implicit explicit

The Thèsez-vous framing, and the single most useful idea in this session: nearly everything that goes wrong here goes wrong because something reasonable was never said out loud.

### Negotiate the ground rules

Thèsez-vous names two essentials to agree explicitly:

1. **The hours you'll devote to each activity** — writing, lab work, teaching, the side project they keep suggesting.
2. **When and how often you'll show progress** — weekly email, monthly meeting, chapter by chapter.

Worth adding: the role of each committee member, what resources you can expect, and which conferences you're aiming for.

### Agree the feedback contract

For the thesis: the dates you'll send drafts, how many versions they'll comment on, their turnaround time — **and yours**. For articles: who drafts first, what each person contributes, and author order.

In writing. Not because you distrust anyone, but because two people remember a hallway conversation differently in eighteen months.

### Own the meeting cycle

You can run this unilaterally; it doesn't need anyone's permission and it changes the quality of supervision immediately.

- **The day before:** send a short agenda — *done / stuck / need from you*.
- **In the meeting:** ask scoped questions. Not "what do you think?" but "does the argument in §2 hold?"
- **Afterwards:** two lines of minutes by email — what was agreed, who does it, by when.

That last one is thirty seconds of typing and it is the single highest-return habit in this whole session. It creates a shared record, it surfaces misunderstandings while they're still small, and it quietly makes deadlines real.

### Keep records, and escalate well

Save the email trail. Raise problems with your supervisor first. Compare notes with other students — you will usually find the thing you thought was uniquely your failing is a known feature. If it doesn't resolve, your postgraduate coordinator or program director exists precisely for this. Knowing your rights isn't betrayal.

## Decoding supervisor-speak

Every vague comment is an invitation to ask a sharper question.

| What they say | What to do with it |
|---|---|
| "This section needs work" | Ask which of **structure, evidence or prose** — make them choose one |
| "Have you considered [entire other field]?" | Ask: "Is that essential for the thesis, or an interesting aside?" Scope creep dies in the open. |
| "Looks fine." | Ask one targeted question anyway. "Fine" often means "didn't read closely." |
| "Why isn't this done yet?" | Show the plan, renegotiate the timeline out loud, and minute the new date. |

### Do this now — 15 minutes

**1. Write the agenda for your next supervision meeting.** Three headings, three bullets each, no more:

```
DONE since last time
  · …
STUCK on
  · …
NEED FROM YOU
  · … (be specific: a decision, a signature, feedback on §2 by Friday)
```

**2. Draft one scoped feedback ask.** Take the thing you're about to send and replace "let me know what you think" with a question that names the section and the kind of feedback you want.

**3. Answer the two Thèsez-vous questions for yourself** — how many hours on what, and how often you'll show progress — then put them on the agenda as something to confirm. If your answer and theirs differ, you've just found the thing that was going to cause trouble in month nine.

## Templates you can steal

**Chasing a silent supervisor** — after two weeks, not four:

> Hi [Name], following up on the draft I sent on [date]. I'm planning to submit the chapter to the committee on [date], so it'd help to have your comments by [date] — even partial ones, or just on §2 if that's easier. If that timing doesn't work, let me know what does and I'll replan around it.

**Two-line minutes** — send within the hour, every time:

> Thanks for the meeting. To confirm what we agreed: I'll [task] by [date]; you'll [task] by [date]. Next meeting [date]. Shout if I've mis-remembered anything.

**Pushing back on scope creep:**

> That's an interesting angle. Before I take it on — is it essential for the thesis argument, or a good idea for a later paper? I'm trying to keep [current chapter] on track for [date], and I'd rather park it deliberately than let it drift.
