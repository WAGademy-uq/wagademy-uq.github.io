## Where does what you read actually go?

That question catches most people out, and the honest answers are recognisable:

- **The infinite pile.** New papers arrive faster than anyone can read them. "Caught up" is not a real state — strategy beats stamina.
- **Read-and-forget.** You read it, highlighted it, and six months later remember nothing. Reading without capture is entertainment.
- **The notes graveyard.** Highlights and PDFs scattered across five apps. Notes you can't find again are notes you never took.
- **No stop criterion.** "I'll start writing when I've read enough." Without a definition of *enough*, the reading never ends.

## The recension pipeline

Four steps, in order. Most literature panic comes from starting at step 3.

### 1. Scope the object before searching

Before you type anything into a database: ask your supervisors and your subject librarian who the must-read authors are. Start from handbooks, review articles and meta-analyses rather than primary papers — they hand you the map.

Build two artefacts as you go: a **personal lexicon** (the terms your field uses, including the ones you keep mixing up) and a **first concept map** (boxes and arrows, on paper, ten minutes).

### 2. Set limits — and write them down

Explicit inclusion and exclusion criteria, decided early and recorded: years (post-2010, say), geography, population, methodology, language.

Write down your keywords and your exclusions *as you go*. Two reasons: your methods section will need exactly this, and without a record you will re-run the same fruitless search in three months.

### 3. Search smart, then snowball

Databases beat Google Scholar for precision — they have controlled vocabularies, thesauri, and proper boolean operators. Learn the syntax for the one database that matters in your field; it takes an afternoon and saves weeks.

Then snowball: found a great source? Mine its reference list, and look at what has cited it since. The French call this *la boule de neige*, and it finds things keyword searches miss.

### 4. Filter in three waves, and capture as you go

Titles → abstracts → full text. Most papers should die at the title stage; that's the system working, not you being hasty.

Use a reference manager (Zotero or Mendeley) **from day one**, not from the day it gets bad. Take reading notes with full references and page numbers attached. Impressions are impossible to reconstruct later — "that paper with the good figure about mice" is not a citation.

## Cataloguing versus arguing

This is the difference between a literature review that reads as competent and one that reads as necessary.

| A review that catalogues | A review that argues |
|---|---|
| "Smith (2020) found X. Jones (2021) found Y. Lee (2022)…" | "Two camps disagree about X; the evidence splits by method…" |
| Organised by paper, in the order you read them | Organised by idea, tension, or gap |
| Shows that you did the reading | Shows why *your* project needs to exist |
| Reads like an annotated bibliography | Every cited paper is doing a job in your argument |

## When to stop

Two defensible criteria — pick whichever fits your project, and say which one you used:

- **Your argument holds.** You can state the gap your work fills, and every claim in that statement is supported.
- **Saturation.** New papers stop adding new categories. You keep finding the same four positions restated.

Neither of these is "I've read everything." Nobody has read everything.

### Do this now — 20 minutes

1. **Write your inclusion and exclusion criteria** for your current review, in five bullet points. Years, population, methods, languages, anything else. Save it in the document you'll eventually write the methods section in.
2. **Open your reference manager** — install Zotero if you don't have one — and file the last five papers you read, with a two-line note each: *what it claims* and *what job it does in my argument*.
3. **Pick your stop criterion** and write it down as a sentence. You will want to have it in writing at 11pm in month eleven.

## Tools worth knowing

- **[Zotero](https://www.zotero.org/)** — free, open source, syncs, and the browser connector saves a paper with one click. The safe default.
- **[Connected Papers](https://www.connectedpapers.com/)** — builds a visual graph of papers related to a seed paper. Excellent for finding what you've missed. Five free graphs a month.
- **[SciSpace](https://scispace.com/)** — AI-assisted reading and literature exploration. Useful for orientation; verify everything it tells you against the actual paper.
- **[Litmaps](https://www.litmaps.com/)** and **[Research Rabbit](https://www.researchrabbit.ai/)** — similar citation-graph tools, both with free tiers.
