## What scares you most about submitting?

- **The black box.** You submit into silence for months. Knowing the pipeline — editor triage, reviewer hunt, decision — makes the wait interpretable rather than ominous.
- **Reviewer 2.** Every field has one: dismissive, demanding, possibly reviewing a different paper. Their comments feel personal. They aren't.
- **Decision-letter shock.** "Major revisions" reads like rejection the first time you see it. It is in fact the modal outcome for papers that eventually get published.
- **The sunk-cost spiral.** Months of revisions on one paper while the thesis waits. Knowing when to comply, push back, or withdraw is a strategic skill nobody teaches.

## The pipeline, demystified

### Choose the journal before you polish

Scope, audience, typical article type, review speed, publication cost. Read the aims and scope properly, and look at three recent papers — if none of them look like yours, that's your answer.

Write the cover letter early, even in draft. It forces you to answer "why this journal?" while you can still act on the answer.

### What reviewers actually check

Less than you fear about prose; more than you'd like about method.

- Does the claim match the evidence? (This is where most papers lose.)
- Are the methods sound, and reported in enough detail to be repeated?
- Is the contribution clear, and is it placed honestly against what's already published?

Elegance of writing matters far less than clarity of claim. A reviewer who has to reconstruct what you did will not be generous.

### The response letter is a genre

Papers are accepted or rejected partly on the quality of the response letter, and almost nobody is taught to write one.

The form is a point-by-point table: **reviewer comment → your response → what changed, with line numbers.** Quote each comment in full, respond to every single one — including the ones you disagree with — and never silently ignore anything.

Three moves are available for each comment: **thank and comply**, **address partially and explain the limit**, or **rebut with evidence**. All three are acceptable. Silence is not.

### Pushing back is allowed

Disagree with a reviewer? Say so, politely, with citations. Editors adjudicate, and a well-argued rebuttal often wins — particularly when two reviewers contradict each other, which happens constantly and which the editor already knows.

## Decision letters, translated

| What the letter says | What it means for you |
|---|---|
| **Reject** | Wrong venue, or wrong week. The feedback is free consulting — reformat and resubmit elsewhere within the month, while it's still fresh. |
| **Major revisions** | The paper is alive. Comply where fair, rebut where not, resubmit. Most papers that get here eventually get published. |
| **Minor revisions** | Champagne-adjacent. Do exactly what's asked, quickly, and change nothing else. |
| **"Reviewer 2 wants six new experiments"** | Do the reasonable subset; argue that the rest is beyond the scope of this paper. Editors know the type. |

Rejection is redirection with free consulting attached.

### Do this now — 20 minutes

**If you have a paper in progress:** pick your target journal and write the two sentences of the cover letter that say *why this journal*. If you can't write them, you have the wrong journal.

**If you have a decision letter sitting unopened** — and a surprising number of people do — open it and build the response table. Just the first column: every comment, quoted in full, numbered. Do not respond yet. Splitting "read the criticism" from "answer the criticism" makes both much easier.

**If neither applies:** find a published paper in your field with an open peer review history ([eLife](https://elifesciences.org/), [PeerJ](https://peerj.com/) and [BMJ Open](https://bmjopen.bmj.com/) all publish theirs) and read the reviews and the authors' response. Half an hour of this demystifies the whole process more than any amount of advice.

## Response letter skeleton

Copy this into a document and fill it in:

> We thank the reviewers for their careful reading and helpful suggestions. We have revised the manuscript accordingly; changes are marked in the tracked-changes file and referenced by line number below.
>
> **Reviewer 1, comment 1.** *"[quote the comment in full]"*
>
> **Response.** We agree, and have [what you did]. See lines 142–158.
>
> **Reviewer 1, comment 2.** *"[quote]"*
>
> **Response.** We have addressed this in part. We have added [X] at lines 201–210. We have not [Y], because [reason, with citation] — we hope the reviewer will agree this is beyond the scope of the present study.

Polite, specific, complete, and easy for a busy editor to check. That's the whole craft.
