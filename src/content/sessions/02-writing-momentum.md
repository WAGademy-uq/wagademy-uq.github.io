## What does your procrastination actually look like?

Worth answering honestly before reading on, because the tactics differ. Four common shapes:

- **Perfectionism.** "It's not ready to be written yet." The bar for a first draft is set at final-draft height, so nothing ever clears it.
- **Blank-page dread.** Starting is the expensive part. A blank document has no handholds — the brain flinches and opens email instead.
- **"I'll read one more paper."** Reading feels like progress and risks nothing. It is the most respectable form of avoidance in academia.
- **Fear of judgment.** The draft feels like it *is* you, so sharing it — even with a supervisor — feels like being graded as a person.

## The myth, and the working reality

| The myth says | The reality is |
|---|---|
| Good writers produce clean prose in one pass | Published authors write bad first drafts and then revise. That *is* the job. |
| You must understand everything before writing anything | Writing is how you find out what you think. Clarity comes out of drafting, not before it. |
| If it's hard, you're not cut out for this | Difficulty is the task, not a verdict on you. |
| More time means better writing | Constraints produce writing; open-ended time produces guilt. |

## Four tactics that restart the engine

### 1. Write an ugly first draft on purpose

Lower the bar until you can step over it. Bullet points instead of sentences. The wrong word left in place with a note. `[CITE LATER]` wherever a reference would slow you down. Write "this paragraph needs to explain why the sample was small" and move on.

You cannot revise a blank page. You can revise almost anything else.

### 2. Snack writing

Fifteen minutes counts. Waiting for a clear day is how a month disappears — and the clear day, when it arrives, is usually spent on email anyway.

Attach small writing sessions to habits that already exist: the first fifteen minutes after you sit down, before opening your inbox. Before lunch. The point is not the word count on any one day, it's that the document stays warm.

### 3. Separate drafting from editing

They use different parts of your attention and they interfere with each other. Draft with the inner critic locked out of the room; invite it back tomorrow, with coffee.

Practically: turn off the squiggly underlines, and don't reread the previous paragraph before writing the next one.

### 4. Park downhill

Stop mid-paragraph. Before closing the laptop, jot the next sentence — or just the next three words — so tomorrow starts rolling instead of cold.

This is the highest-return habit on the list and it costs about twenty seconds.

<div class="callout do">

### Do this now — 15 minutes

Set a timer for fifteen minutes and write the ugliest possible version of one section you've been avoiding.

Rules: no deleting, no rereading, no looking anything up. If you don't know a fact, write `[CHECK]` and keep going. If a sentence won't come out right, write it wrong.

At the end, don't judge it — just note the word count and where you stopped. Then write the next sentence's first three words and close the laptop. That's parking downhill.

## Getting feedback without dread

The fear-of-judgment version of procrastination doesn't respond to writing tactics. It responds to changing the ask.

Instead of sending a draft with "let me know what you think" — which invites judgment of everything, including you — send it with a scoped question:

- "Does the argument in §2 hold, or is there a step missing?"
- "This is a rough first pass, I know the prose is loose — I'd like feedback on structure only."
- "I'm unsure about the framing in the introduction. The rest can wait."

A scoped ask gets you more useful feedback *and* makes the draft feel less like a verdict. There's more on this in [Session 5](/sessions/5/).
