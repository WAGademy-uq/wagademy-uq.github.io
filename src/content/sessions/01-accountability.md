## A PhD is a productivity trap

Nobody designed it this way. But the ordinary conditions of higher-degree research are almost exactly the conditions that stall work:

- **No real deadlines.** Milestones are months apart. Nothing is due tomorrow, so nothing happens today.
- **Isolation.** You work alone, on a problem nobody else is working on, often from home.
- **Slow feedback.** Weeks between supervisor meetings. Effort and reward are completely decoupled.
- **Perfectionism.** "I'll write once I've read enough" — and the reading is never enough.

If you have been quietly assuming this is a personal failing, it isn't. It's structural. Which is good news, because structures can be changed.

## The WAG loop

A Working Accountability Group adds back the three things a PhD removes: deadlines, witnesses, and feedback. The whole method is three steps, repeated.

1. **Commit.** Say your session goals out loud, and write them somewhere visible.
2. **Work.** Three 45–50 minute pomodoros, phones away, alongside other people.
3. **Report.** Did you hit it? What got in the way? No judgment — it's data.

That's the entire trick. The reporting from one session feeds the commitment of the next.

## Why saying it out loud works

Four separate effects stack up here, and none of them requires you to be more disciplined than you already are:

- **Social facilitation.** People work measurably harder when others are present — even when nobody is watching or speaking.
- **Implementation intentions.** Deciding *when, where and what* beats a vague intention to "work more" by a wide margin.
- **Public commitment.** A goal someone else heard is much harder to quietly drop.
- **Timeboxing.** Fifty focused minutes beats three hours of willpower. The breaks are part of the method, not a concession.

> In Gail Matthews' study at Dominican University, goal achievement rose from around 43% for people who merely thought about their goals to around 76% for those who wrote them down, committed to action, and sent weekly progress reports to a friend.

## Writing objectives that actually work

This is the part most people get wrong, and it is the difference between a productive session and a vague one.

**Vague goals fail invisibly.** "Work on my thesis." "Read some papers." "Do analysis." At the end of the session you can't say whether you succeeded, so you learn nothing and feel bad anyway.

**Specific goals are checkable.** "Draft 300 words of the methods intro." "Read and take notes on three papers about X." "Code the data-cleaning step for dataset Y."

### The formula

> **VERB** + **DELIVERABLE** + **SIZE**

- **Verb** — draft, code, plot, outline, revise, annotate
- **Deliverable** — the thing a stranger could point at
- **Size** — sized to fit one pomodoro of 45–50 minutes

*"Revise two paragraphs of discussion §4.2 using Tuesday's feedback."*

**The stranger test:** could someone who isn't you verify it got done? If not, sharpen it.

### Verb bank, by phase

Adapted from Thèsez-vous. Pick the row that matches where you actually are — a lot of stalled writing is someone trying to *write* when they are still in *explore*.

| Phase | Verbs |
|---|---|
| Explore | list · note · annotate · define · sketch |
| Review | identify · classify · summarise · link |
| Pre-write | plan · scope · order · outline |
| Write | introduce · elaborate · illustrate · conclude |
| Rewrite | reformulate · question · compare · tighten |
| Revise | check · correct · standardise |

### Do this now — 5 minutes

Open a blank note and write three objectives for your next work session, one per pomodoro, each in **verb + deliverable + size** form.

Then run each through the stranger test. If you can't say yes-or-no whether it's done, rewrite it. Most first attempts fail this test — that's normal, and catching it now is the whole point.

Add one more line: **my distraction plan** — what you'll do when you're tempted to check email. ("Write the thought on a sticky and keep going" works better than "resist.")

## Making it visible

In the live sessions we use a shared Miro board with three columns — **To do**, **Ongoing**, **Done** — and everyone claims a sticky colour for the whole series. One sticky per objective, dragged across as the session goes.

It sounds trivial. It isn't: moving a sticky to Done is a small, real reward at the exact moment the work finishes, and after six weeks the board is a visible log of everything the group shipped.

Working alone, a paper sticky note on the edge of your screen does the same job. The requirement is only that the goals are **visible and moved by hand** — not buried in a task app you'll stop opening.
